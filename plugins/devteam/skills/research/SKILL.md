# research

Research the slice the product serves, not the container it sits in. A country, an industry or a whole city is a container. "Foreign tourists at surf and dive hubs who need a nurse" is a slice. Numbers about people who would never use the product are noise, however big.

## 1. Write the scope line before the first search

One line, from the project's own docs or memory, not guessed:

> Who is served · where they are · what they do with the product · the question to answer.

If the product model is unclear, ask one question. Don't research a model you invented. If the user corrects the model mid-way, rewrite the scope line and redo the searches it affects.

## 2. Pick the unit of analysis from the slice

- Use the unit the customer experiences: a destination, a hub, a neighborhood, a customer segment. Not the country.
- A country figure is allowed once, as a one-line context note. It is never the headline and never the basis of a recommendation.
- If the slice-level number doesn't exist, say so and give the closest proxy, labelled as a proxy. Don't fall back to the container's number.

## 3. Build the searches from the slice

For each candidate place or segment, cover the same six facets, so comparisons are fair:

1. **Demand in the slice**: how many of the right people, and how many are foreign, paying or repeat.
2. **The gap**: evidence the need is unmet there (access, wait, distance, price).
3. **Who already serves it**: the providers a customer would use instead, and how they work.
4. **Rules for the service as offered**: licensing, scope of practice, data privacy, for the exact thing sold.
5. **Operating constraints**: ownership caps, entity, payments, language, seasonality.
6. **Partners**: who could host or license the work where a rule blocks going alone.

Run independent searches in parallel. A facet with no result is a finding: record it as not found.

## 4. Source discipline

- Prefer official statistics, regulators, and law-firm or ministry guides. Provider and vendor pages are marketing claims; say so.
- Tag every finding **Sourced** (a link supports it), **Judgment** (your inference, with the reasoning), or **Unverified** (plausible, not checked).
- When sources disagree, say they disagree and leave the figure out, or show the range.
- Never fill a gap with a guess or a remembered number. List what was not researched as plainly as what was.
- Check dates. A 2018 article is not today's rule.

## 5. Shape the answer

1. The recommendation, in one or two lines.
2. Slice-level evidence, as a table by place with the six facets as columns.
3. For expansion questions: an ordered ladder of waves. Each wave names why it's next and the condition that must be true before it starts (a pilot result, a licence answer, a partner).
4. Gaps: what is unverified and the cheapest way to close each.

Use the project's own report format if it has one. Keep the sourced facts and the judgment visibly apart.

## Worked example: a nurse app for tourists

Scope line: *foreign tourists at island surf and dive hubs, who go to a nurse for triage and minor care; is Thailand a better first market than the Philippines?*

- **Wrong:** "Thailand had 33M international arrivals." That counts tourists in Bangkok and Chiang Mai who will never use the app.
- **Right:** foreign arrivals at Siargao, Boracay, Phuket, Samui and Phangan; evidence of a care gap there; which clinics already send staff to hotels; whether a foreign-trained nurse may practise there and who may own the company.
- **Expansion:** the same six facets for each next hub, then a ladder: nearby hubs under the same licence first, then English-speaking hubs through a licensed local partner, then markets where a rule or competitor needs more research.

## Before you answer

Reread the scope line and cut any number or claim that is about someone outside it.
